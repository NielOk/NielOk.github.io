Some notes so I don't forget.

  • On government
    The two-party system in democracy has caused the government to bloat itself with powers that aren't necessary for its core function of protecting the rights of citizens. By turning elections into popularity contests, the government has burdened itself with controlling the minutiae of society, many of which are better handled by individuals or private organizations. By burdening itself with these responsibilities, it actively distracts citizens from voting on policies that actually affect their rights.
    Deficit spending has allowed citizens to constantly vote yes to policies that they would vote no on if they actually had to pay for them. Who doesn't want free stuff? It encourages politicians to create programs that further burden the government with responsibilities it can't support with its current tax revenue in order to get the votes of citizens who would be benefitting without cost to themselves. What happens when the government can no longer borrow money because of a lack of investor faith in its ability to pay back its debts? The government will have to raise taxes to support its bloated responsibilities, coming out of the pockets of citizens who didn't understand the costs of the policies they voted for. Or it will have to ruthlessly cut programs that citizens may now depend on.
    Democracy is probably the correct machinery for a government to be responsive to its citizens and change its policies to reflect the will of the people without the necessity of violent revolution. Hopefully the times of having to constantly overthrow governments for the sake of change are behind us. However, there needs to be a clear definition of what are considered a citizen's rights that a government needs to protect. It feels as though in modern politics we forget this. Importantly, citizens need to be willing to pay for protection of those rights. The social contract, as in what the government is expected to do for its citizens and in turn what citizens sacrifice in return, needs to be more clearly defined and elections need to reflect the importance of these fundamental rights and the possible redefinition of those rights rather than surface level discussions on policy. Each policy needs to be clear on which rights we are protecting. Also, I personally believe that the fundamental rights of citizens don't change super often over time, so there needs to be a mechanism to prevent the government from constantly changing the definition of rights to suit its own needs.
    I'm not sure how we would go about making these changes in the government. The best would be the opportunity to write a government from scratch, but there are rarely such opportunities. We would somehow have to work within the existing system to make these changes. If we were given the opportunity to write one from scratch, we must understand why the Founding Fathers failed to build in these mechanisms, as I'm sure they were aware of the potential for these problems. George Washington warned against political parties, there were many debates on the definition of rights, yet the government was still built in a way that allowed for these problems to arise.
  • On significance
    Life has no inherent meaning, but moments in our lives can be significant to us because we are human beings who change based on our experiences. The universe doesn't care about our experiences, but we do. Searching for experiences that change us in a way that we find significant is a worthwhile pursuit, as well as choosing how we want to change based on our experiences.
  • On free will
    On a mechanical level we probably don't have free will, and everything that we do is determined by a complex set of factors beyond our control. My problem with this idea is that it's true, but it's pretty useless as a model of human behavior. It's much more useful to assume that we have free will and make decisions based on that assumption. If we don't, accountability and justice become meaningless and a functional society becomes impossible. On an individual level, believing that we have free will allows us to put more care into our decisions. We probably evolved to believe that we have free will because it is part of a useful, compact model of our behavior.
  • On compression
    Somehow the universe has organized itself in a manner that allows for compressed glimpses of its history to be visible to any keen observer. Light from distant stars, fossilized remains, human memories and written records, music, and any object that is perceivable to an observer (like a bottle of soap), are all examples of this. This is remarkable and I don't even know yet how to begin to formulate a useful question on why this is the case.
  • On forgiveness
    What's the point of allowing pain from the past to continue to affect you in the present? Take what you learned from the experience and move on. Pain is a great teacher, but holding on to it after you learn just extends your suffering. It's your choice on whether or not you want the person that hurt you to continue to be a part of your life. I personally choose to keep people in my life if I believe they will change for the better. I don't need an apology I just need them to change their behavior. But I don't always choose to keep people in my life because you can take a look at someone's values and their situation and see that they're not likely to change.
  • On uncertainty
    Science and philosophy are cool because they taught us that it is okay to sit in uncertainty while simultaneously searching for truth. Just because we don't know everything doesn't mean we have to try to fill in the gaps with assumptions. Having a standard for what we consider to be knowledge and truth helps us to stay grounded in reality. I will admit not everything has an objective truth to find in the first place, but there are at least some things that we can say are objectively true and it is worth searching for them. Searching for the boundary of what can be known is also worthwhile.
