What makes a game a good test of intelligence?

Not all games seem to reward intelligence equally. In some games, it's very easy to reach the ceiling of performance. In Tic-Tac-Toe, for example, nearly every game ends in a draw once both players understand the basic strategy. Even if I played against my kindergarten self, it would likely still end in a draw. Once a minimal level of competence is reached, improvements in decision-making no longer translate into better outcomes.

In others, like chess or Go, we have a very different situation. Better decision-making consistently leads to better outcomes, and even small improvements in play can meaningfully change results. The game is highly sensitive to the quality of decisions: stronger policies reliably outperform weaker ones.

Then there are games like poker, where even optimal play doesn't guarantee a win. I could consistently make better decisions than a weaker opponent and still lose individual hands because the outcome depends on random factors like the cards dealt. In this case, outcomes are a noisy reflection of decision quality rather than a direct measure of it.

These games differ in how sensitive their outcomes are to decision quality of the player. What exactly determines this sensitivity?

Separating skill from luck

The examples above suggest that not all variation in outcomes comes from differences in intelligence. In some games, better decisions translate cleanly into better results. In others, outcomes are partially obscured by randomness. To understand this difference, we need to separate what a player controls from what they do not.

We can model a player as operating according to a policy, which we define as a mapping from game states to actions. I define the strength of a policy as its expected performance against a specified opponent or distribution of opponents.

I want to separately define policy update strength, which captures how quickly a player can improve their policy with experience. In this section, I will focus only on policy strength and return to learning later.

Given fixed policies, the outcome of a game is not always deterministic. Even if two players follow identical policies, the result can vary due to things like initial conditions and hidden information. So, we can model the outcome of a game as

\[ R = f(\pi, \pi', \omega) \]

where, \( R \) is the outcome/reward, \( \pi \) is the player's policy, \( \pi' \) represents the policies of other players, and \( \omega \) captures stochastic elements of the game such as initial conditions, random draws (e.g. shuffled cards), and other sources of randomness.

Note that hidden actions are not treated as stochastic elements since they are decisions sampled from the other players' policies that are hidden from the player only because they aren't observable.

Crucially, \( \pi' \) captures the strategic behavior of other agents, while \( \omega \) captures uncertainty that is not influenced by any player’s decisions.

Without this distinction, we can't tell whether differences in outcomes are due to better decisions or unavoidable randomness.

This decomposition lets us ask a more precise question: how much of the variation in outcomes is actually caused by differences in policy?

To make this precise, consider the expected performance of a policy against a fixed opponent:

\[ V(\pi | \pi') = \mathbb{E}_{\omega}[f(\pi, \pi', \omega)] \]

This represents the average outcome a player can expect when following policy π, with randomness averaged out. By taking this expectation, we isolate the effect of decision-making from the noise introduced by stochastic elements of the game. The remaining question is how large this effect is relative to the noise introduced by randomness.
